Analytics Mindset: Metrics and In-Depth Analysis

Choose the right metrics, slice them with the right dimensions, check the overall number against its detail, find what drives a change, and estimate the impact of a decision. Practice on AdventureWorks.

A number alone is not an answer. Pick metrics that show value, slice them with a purpose, and check the overall figure against its detail before you tell anyone what it means. Try the workbench in tab 3.

Part 1 Define the right key metrics

IdeaWhat it isExample
North Star Metric (NSM)The one number the whole company focuses on for long-term growth, over years. When it rises, revenue-related metrics follow.Bill pay service: monthly active paying users
One Metric That Matters (OMTM)The number one team focuses on for a period, depending on the product stage.Months 1 to 6: new users and CAC. Months 7 to 12: churn rate
Output metricAn outcome or result.Revenue, active customers
Input metricAn action or driver you can influence.New customers, orders per customer, order value

The slides call revenue the worst example of a North Star. Revenue is what customers pay. The NSM should be the value they get back for it. A customer who feels the value pays more over time, so more sales is the result of a growing NSM. To find input metrics for an output, split it with the MECE math approach: Revenue = Customers x Orders per customer x Order value.

A way to cut a company into stages and see where to focus: acquisition, activation, retention, referral and revenue. The order of the stages differs between business models.

Part 2 Metrics and dimensions

MetricDimension
It isWhat you measureHow you slice it
ExamplesRevenue, cost, orders, churn rate, delivery timeYear, month, channel, country, category, segment
TestIf adding two rows makes sense, it is a metric.If it names a row instead of measuring it, it is a dimension.

"Revenue was $55.8M" is true and impossible to act on. The same metric by year, then by month, then by channel is where it turns into knowledge. This is the dimension ladder: year asks "are we growing?", year and month asks "is it seasonal?", channel and month asks "what is driving it?"

  • Hierarchy (country, city, district; year, quarter, month): drill one level at a time. Skipping a level hides where the problem sits.
  • Flat dimensions (channel, payment method, segment): cross them freely with each other and with any level.
  • How many groups? 2 to 12 is readable on a slide. 20 to 200 needs a top N and an "other" bucket. Thousands means you grouped by a key, such as order ID, and got the raw table back.
  • "City x channel" is a question. "City x district x SKU x campaign" is a data dump.

Part 3 Overall against detail

Given the overall view, does the detail follow the same trend? Read in layers: overall, then by one dimension, then by two or three. Where a layer disagrees with the one above, that is the finding.

Overall against distribution. An average can look healthy while the tail is not. In a delivery example on the slides, the average is 28 minutes and the 90th percentile is 52, so one order in ten takes nearly twice as long, and that is where refunds come from.

Part 4 Key factors and root cause

StepWhat to do
1. Rule out a data problemTracking changed (a release stopped events), definition changed (metric logic, filter, join), rows missing or late (check row counts per source per day), scope changed (new market, test group, filter left on). If one dimension goes to exactly zero, or the break lands on a release date, stop analysing and check the pipeline.
2. The 80/20 checkA few causes make up most of the result. Work where the mass is: a 5% gain in a big category beats doubling a tiny one.
3. If 80/20 comes back flatChange the dimension, look for a shared cause upstream of all segments, or accept that the lever is system-wide. A flat Pareto is a finding.
4. 5 WhysAsk why until the answer is something you control. Each answer must be checkable in the data. Run two or three chains for a big problem.
5. Decomposition treeBreak a total down one dimension at a time (a visual in Power BI) to see where the mass sits.
6. Compared to what?At every why, name the group that did not change. If only one district is late, a district-specific cause is a real candidate. If all moved, the cause is wider.

Where 5 Whys goes wrong: stopping at a symptom, naming a person, accepting unchecked answers, following only one chain.

Part 5 Impact estimation

What happens if we change a price, run a tactic or launch a product? The input is historical data, the insights so far, and assumptions written down. The output is a simulation that lets the business refine the decision before committing money.

The slide example: free shipping above $35 lifts the basket, but the result hangs on one assumption. Below a certain basket lift, the scenario loses money. State that threshold.

Watch out Mistakes in a first deep dive

  • Revenue as the North Star. It measures what you collect, not the value customers get.
  • A total that hides a mix shift. Order value fell 44.6% in 2014 overall, but only 10.4% once the channel mix is held at its 2013 level (Practice 2).
  • An average over a long tail. 4.8% of orders bring 64.5% of revenue (Practice 2).
  • Analysing a drop before checking the data. June 2014 revenue is $49,006 against $5.4M in May, and Bikes and Components stop appearing on 31 May (Practice 3).
  • A scenario with one hidden assumption. A 10% discount needs 19.0% more units just to break even on gross profit (Practice 4).
  • Part years read as full years. 2011 and 2014 each hold only part of a year, so compare like with like.
