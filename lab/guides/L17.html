Data Storytelling and Bias in Analytics

Present an analysis so people act on it, then challenge it for bias before you share it. Practice on the CRM pipeline and AdventureWorks.

The last step of an analysis decides whether anyone uses it. Lead with the answer, show only what supports it, and check your own bias before you present. Try the workbench in tab 3.

Why The Present step

The result of an analytics project is usually a deck of findings, key insights and recommendations. It is the first thing the audience sees, and they judge your expertise by it before they look at any technical detail. They rarely care what you did in the earlier steps. Presenting well is what decides whether the project succeeds.

Structure A typical analytics deck

#BlockIt holds
1Project overview and business contextPurpose, objectives, where the project sits in the business.
2Datasets overviewSources, types of data, characteristics. It shows how far the insights can be trusted.
3Terms and metrics definitionThe key terms used, so everyone reads the numbers the same way.
4ApproachesThe methods and techniques used.
5Executive summaryThe key findings in a short overview, for decision-makers.
6RecommendationSpecific steps to fix the problems or use the opportunities.
7Detailed analysisFindings, visuals and interpretation, for the audience who wants to explore.

Readers enter at different depths. A busy executive gets the executive summary and the recommendation in 2 minutes. A 10-minute reader also needs the approaches and the metric definitions so everyone is on the same page. A deep-dive reader wants the datasets and the detailed analysis. Answer first, evidence after: an executive should get the point by slide 2.

The Pyramid Principle orders the content the same way: the answer at the top, then the arguments that support it, then the data under each argument.

Idea Data storytelling = data + narrative + visuals

PartIts jobAlone, it gives
DataThe what: facts and numbers you can trust.A table nobody reads.
VisualsSee it in 3 seconds.A chart without a reason to care.
NarrativeThe why and the so what: context, insight, next step.An opinion without proof.

Data plus narrative explains, data plus visuals enlightens, narrative plus visuals engages. All three together move people to decide and act. The slides show the same bookings numbers three times: a table, then a line chart, then the line with a caption: "Cashback launched in April: bookings +32% MoM, but growth has stalled since. Optimize voucher cost next."

Narrative The 4-step arc

  1. Hook. The situation that made someone ask.
  2. Rising. The evidence that builds the insight.
  3. Aha. The finding that explains it.
  4. Next. The action to take.

Start with the hook, not the method. Tell the insight, not the chronology of your analysis.

Data Three rules for the numbers

  • Rates beat totals. Revenue per customer says more than revenue.
  • Remove surplus. If it does not support the point, take it out. Show 3 growth routes, put the other 7 in the appendix.
  • Aggregate the long tail. Top few plus "Other".

Visuals Make the chart carry the message

Your questionChart
Compare categoriesBar chart
Trend over timeLine graph
Share of a whole, few partsPie or donut
Spread of one metricHistogram or box plot
Relationship of two metricsScatter plot
Intensity across two dimensionsHeatmap
  • One chart, one message. Make that comparison the easiest one. Stacked bars make only the totals easy to read.
  • Group what must be compared. Bars to compare sit next to each other.
  • Focus attention. Grey out the noise, use one highlight colour, annotate the why on the chart. If everything is coloured, nothing is highlighted.

Consultant style One slide, one takeaway

The title is the main message of the slide, written as a sentence. Under it sit 2 or 3 takeaways that support the message, each with a number, and the chart on which the audience should focus. A closing line says what to do about it.

Example from the slides: "Online channel accounted for about 20% of total revenue", "its share rose from about 16% to about 22%", "customers are gradually switching from offline to online, so long-term growth should focus on online".

Bias What it is and where it enters

Bias is an incline toward or against something. In analytics it is an incline in how we ask, collect or read data. It is not a sign of a bad analyst: it is the default setting of every brain and every dataset. The job is to catch it before the decision, not after.

StageBias that enters
1. Ask (hypothesis, question)Confirmation
2. Collect (data, sample)Sampling, survivorship
3. Analyze (numbers, metrics)Outliers, Simpson's paradox
4. Present (charts, narrative)Framing
5. Decide (act on insight)Availability

Cheat sheet Seven biases and the question that catches each

BiasFamilyIn plain wordsRed-flag questionFix
ConfirmationCognitiveSeeking proof, not truthDid I look for evidence against my idea?List alternative causes. Test the opposite.
FramingCognitiveSame fact, different wrapperWould I decide the same if it were worded the opposite way?Show MoM, YoY and vs KPI, absolute and relative.
AvailabilityCognitiveThe loudest story winsIs this common, or just memorable?Use base rates and full-period data.
SamplingStatisticalThe sample is not the populationWho is in my sample, and who is not?Random or stratified sample, tests of 2+ weeks.
SurvivorshipStatisticalOnly winners are visibleWho is missing from this data?Include churned and failed cases.
OutliersStatisticalOne extreme drags the averageDoes the median tell the same story as the mean?Check the distribution. Report mean and median.
Simpson's paradoxStatisticalGroup trends flip when combinedDoes the trend hold in every segment?Segment first, then aggregate.

Checklist Six questions before you share

  1. Alternatives. Did I list explanations other than my favourite? (confirmation)
  2. Comparisons. Did I show MoM, YoY, vs KPI, absolute and percent? (framing)
  3. Base rate. Is it backed by data, or is it a memorable story? (availability)
  4. Sample. Does my data cover all user types and enough time? (sampling)
  5. Missing. Who dropped out of the dataset: churned, failed, silent? (survivorship)
  6. Shape. Did I check the median, outliers and segment-level trends? (outliers and Simpson's)

Watch out Mistakes in a first presentation

  • A leader board of totals. The agent with the most won value is 18th on win rate (Practice 1).
  • An average that describes nobody. The average order is $3,491 and the median is $783 (Practice 2).
  • Only the winners in the data. Won deals average $2,361. Across all closed deals the average is $1,491 (Practice 2).
  • A headline that depends on the wrapper. The same 2014 revenue reads -54.0% or +7.4% (Practice 2).
  • A slide that proves nothing without a number. Each takeaway needs a figure and a period (Practice 3).
  • A finding presented before it is challenged. Run the six questions on your own result first (Practice 4).
